## Preamble: Systems Science, An Underestimated Foundational Science

When we discuss the "foundational" nature of science, we traditionally follow the hierarchical reductionist ladder downwards: particles are more foundational than atoms, atoms than molecules, molecules than cells. Within this default metric, systems science—which studies holistic laws—has naturally been categorized as an "interdisciplinary applied subject," as if it were merely a derivative output of physics, biology, or sociology standing on the shoulders of primary sciences.

However, this is an epistemological inversion that obscures the profound, paradigm-shifting breakthrough occurring in systems science—a breakthrough capable of reconstructing humanity's understanding of order.

### I. Misplaced Positioning: The Reductionist Framework Cannot Fit the Dimension of Systems Science

Today’s mainstream academic classification system is fundamentally a product of reductionism.

For 400 years, the reductionist methodology has achieved overwhelming success: decomposing complex wholes into simple units, and deriving holistic behavior by studying local laws. This paradigm birthed classical mechanics, quantum mechanics, and molecular biology, underpinning modern industrial civilization. Consequently, we defaulted to a hierarchy of value: the closer a discipline is to the microscopic physical substrate, the more "foundational" it is; the more it studies macroscopic wholes, the more "applied" it becomes.

Yet this hierarchy narrow-mindedly restricts the definition of "foundational science" and long conceals the true value of systems science.

The core hallmark of a foundational science is never "studying the smallest unit," but "discovering the most universal laws." The Second Law of Thermodynamics is a core law of foundational science not because it studies microscopic particles, but because it governs all energy conversion processes—from heat engines to stars, from life to civilization—without exception.

What systems science reveals is a meta-law even broader than thermodynamics: **it governs the generation, persistence, and decay of all order.**

Reductionist physical laws answer *how matter moves*; systemic holistic laws answer *how order is generated*. The former applies to all matter but covers only one facet of reality; the latter applies to all entities possessing whole structures, spanning matter, life, society, and cognition across all scales. We can state that physical laws are special cases of systemic laws under simple closed systems, whereas systemic laws are the extension of physical laws in complex open systems.

More crucially, past systems research, even when emphasizing the whole, unconsciously retained reductionist habits—treating the "whole" as a giant single object to dissect, model, and search for centralized control logic. This failed to touch the true kernel of systemic persistence. The genuine breakthrough of modern systems science lies in leaping out of centralized local vision, starting instead from the structural transport of information and mass-energy to reconstruct the global state transition equations.

Excluding systems science from foundational science simply because it does not study microscopic particles is equivalent to claiming General Relativity is less foundational than Newtonian mechanics because it does not study low-speed daily motion. This is not objective academic evaluation, but path dependency of an outdated cognitive framework.

The foundational status of systems science has always existed. Our academic classification system simply has not yet caught up with the dimension it is reaching.

### II. Inheritance and Dimension Elevation: Reductionism as the Foundation, System Theory as Cognitive Evolution

Discussing the value of systems science never requires negating reductionism. Their relationship mirrors Newtonian mechanics and relativity—the former is the foundation of the latter; the latter is the dimension elevation of the former. Reductionism disassembled the world for us to inspect every gear. This is the fundamental prerequisite for systems science to become a "hard science" rather than mysticism. Whole-ism without dissection is merely vague philosophical speculation; with the reductionist substrate, systems science becomes a rigorous discipline that is verifiable, derivable, and deployable.

However, when reductionism reaches its extreme, it inevitably hits its own boundary.

This is not because we have not researched finely enough, but because the whole possesses independent laws that cannot be derived or reduced from local parts.

Systems science addresses precisely this other half of reality untouched by reductionism.

It negates no reductionist conclusions; it merely informs us that the world contains more than one perspective of dissection, and precision does not equal complete truth. When we shift from *studying parts* to *studying wholes*, and from *explaining order* to *constructing order*, we discover another set of equally rigorous, equally universal laws that together complete the full picture of reality.

This is the inevitable evolution of human cognition. Just as history recognized Einstein without negating Newton, future history will acknowledge the foundational status of systems science while remembering the foundation laid by reductionism. Civilization never advances by destroying its predecessors, but by standing on their shoulders to behold a vaster cosmos.
